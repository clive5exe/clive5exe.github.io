FIRSTPRESS: a direct-to-fan music marketplace

The idea

Streaming broke the deal music used to have. An artist earns roughly a third of a cent per stream, so you need hundreds of thousands of plays to make rent. A fan pays a monthly fee and owns nothing, the moment they stop paying their whole library is gone. The value pools in the middle, at the platform and the labels, and the two groups who actually matter, the people making the music and the people who love it, both get the thin end.

FIRSTPRESS is a direct-to-fan marketplace built to undo that. A fan buys a record and owns it, files they download and keep, even if the platform vanishes. The artist keeps 90 percent of every sale and the money hits their bank daily, not on some quarterly royalty statement. No label gate, no algorithm deciding who gets heard, no exclusivity contract. Direct, the way it should have stayed.

It is a two-sided thing on purpose. Fans get discovery, ownership, and a real line to the artists they back. Artists get a storefront, fast money, and the customer relationship that labels normally rent back to them. The whole technical build exists to serve that one sentence. Music deserves a better deal.

The front door

The home page has one job, which is to say what the deal is before anyone has to click anything. Your price, your fans, your money the next day.

Further down it stops selling and starts showing. Everything an artist gets is switched on the day they sign up, and there is no upgrade path to hide behind, so the honest thing is to lay the whole surface out at once.

Browse and discovery

Browse is a catalogue, not a feed. Filter by format across singles, EPs, albums and videos, then by genre. Nothing on it is ranked by who paid to be ranked, which sounds like a small thing until you notice how much of the rest of the internet is.

The artist directory is the other half of discovery. A rotating feature at the top, then the full roster in a grid or a list, sorted newest first or by whatever else you want.

The artist profile

An artist page is the actual product for a lot of people. Hero image, name, follow and share, then three tabs. Music is the catalogue. About is the bio. Community is the fanclub. The shop and the shows live in the same place too, so somebody who came for the album finds the shirt and the show without leaving.

The release page

The release page is where the money actually happens. Artwork, tracklist with real per-track metadata, and a purchase panel that supports a fixed price, free, or pay-what-you-want with a floor the artist sets. Checkout is one screen on Stripe, and access is granted the moment it settles.

Every track carries its format, bitrate, sample rate and BPM on the row. Audio sells as 320kbps MP3, downloaded and kept, and it streams from the same library. There is no subscription that can take it back.

What a fan actually owns

The library is the counterpart to the release page. Everything bought, filtered by music or video, sorted however. Alongside it sit tickets, orders and fanclub memberships, because from the fan’s side those are all the same thing: proof they backed somebody.

There is a native iPhone app for the fan side as well, built in SwiftUI rather than wrapped, because playback and the purchase flow are the two things that feel wrong immediately if they are not native.

The artist dashboard

This is the biggest surface in the product by some distance. It opens on an overview with onboarding state, because an artist who has not connected a payout account needs to know that before they need anything else, and a glance row of releases, page views, revenue and sales.

The left rail is the whole business grouped into four blocks. Workspace holds the overview, releases and events. Storefront holds the artist page, the fanclub and the shop. Business holds payouts, promo codes, marketing and analytics. Account holds notifications, settings and help. That grouping is deliberate, because those four things fail in different ways and get looked at on different days.

Releases

The release manager runs a pipeline of drafts, scheduled and live, with a per-item status and a publish date. Music and video are separate sub-sections because they have different upload paths. Video gets transcoded by a worker pool, which is the slowest thing in the product and the one place a job queue was unavoidable.

The artist page editor

Identity, page address, hero and social links, each editable with a live preview that updates as you type and can be flipped between desktop and mobile. The preview is the real component the public page renders, not a mock of it, which removes an entire category of “it looked fine in the editor” bug.

Merch, with nothing in a warehouse

Merch is print on demand, and it is the part of the build I expected to be simple and was not. Thirteen real garments from four suppliers, from a 146 gsm fitted tee up to a 475 gsm oversized crew, every one printed DTF when somebody buys it. No stock, no minimum, nothing up front.

Creating one is a four-step wizard: pick the body, pick the blank, place your art, set the price and publish. The art is placed in the real print area with the real dimensions rather than dropped onto a flattering mockup, so what an artist approves is what gets printed.

The public product page is the payoff. Every colourway shot on the actual garment, sizes that say what they measure, and fabric, weight, fit and print method written out where people look for them, because the questions that kill a merch sale are all answerable in advance.

The artist sets the retail price and keeps the difference between that and what the blank and the printing cost. There is no platform cut stacked on top of merch, and shipping and replacements are handled on our side.

Events and the door

Tickets sell from the same page as the record. An event carries a poster at its own size, a venue, a date, and as many tiers as the night needs. Every tier has four numbers: a price, a capacity, a per-order limit and a sale window, set once and then the counter runs itself.

The ticket is an Apple Wallet pass with the QR on the front, issued at checkout. Refund it and the pass stops working immediately on the phone it is already sitting on.

The door runs on a second native iPhone app. Staff are invited by email and get access to one show and nothing else, never the money. It refuses already-scanned, refunded, voided and wrong-night tickets with a distinct answer for each, so the person on the door knows what to say. It keeps scanning with no signal and reconciles when the phone gets a bar back, because venue wifi is reliably the first thing to fail. Live check-in counts break down by tier so you know whether to hold the doors.

The fee is a flat dollar per paid ticket. Free tickets cost nothing, and nothing is added to what the fan pays at checkout.

The fanclub

Every artist gets one the day they sign up, free to open, free to run, free to join, and we take nothing out of it. It is a tab on the artist page rather than a separate app, which is the whole point.

Posts can be text, voice, video, a poll of up to six options, or an image. There are reactions for the people who will never type a reply, RSVP-able dates, outbound links and saved posts. Access has three settings, open to anyone, readable by anyone but only buyers can participate, or buyers only, and who can read is a separate question from who can reply. One person can be blocked without changing anything for everybody else. Every post and reply is screened automatically the moment it is written, so the worst of it never reaches the room, and fans can report anything that gets through.

Analytics that name people

The whole dashboard is free from the first visitor, with nothing held back behind a plan. Six tabs: overview, audience, fans, tracks, money and merch, across roughly thirty distinct signals.

The part that justifies building it is the fans tab. A play is a stranger, but a buyer has a name, an order count and a lifetime total. Every buyer is listed and ranked by spend, tiered into a top 5, 10 and 25 percent, and the list keeps loading past five hundred rows rather than showing a flattering top few. Repeat purchasers get their own view with average order value and first and last order dates. And there is an at-risk list of people who own records and have not pressed play in thirty days, because a collection nobody opens is a fan about to leave.

Attribution is last-touch with the first-touch timestamp kept behind it, so revenue lands next to the channel that earned it instead of in one pile marked traffic. There is deliberately no device or browser breakdown and no way to watch what an individual is playing right now. There is also no export button, so the fan list cannot walk out in a spreadsheet, ours included.

Payouts

Earnings accrue whether or not a payout account exists, and the screen says so plainly rather than hiding a balance until setup is done. Pending balance, total paid out, ninety days of earnings and a full payout history.

Promo codes and marketing

Promo codes are per-artist rather than platform-wide, so a discount comes out of the artist’s own margin and nobody else’s. The marketing section is link tooling, including a UTM builder that writes tags the analytics side already knows how to read, which is the difference between attribution working and attribution being a nice idea.

Vinyl

The vinyl calculator is the piece I am fondest of, because it tells artists something the industry generally will not. Pick a size, speed, disc count, run length and weight, and every option shows what it does to the price per copy as you pick it. The estimate breaks down into records, packaging and print, mastering and cut, and the platform’s share, all visible.

It also tells you the unglamorous truths. A hundred copies is the floor because press time is per record and the setup has to spread across enough of them. Bass below about 150 hertz has to be summed to mono for a vinyl cut or the needle jumps. Print files need 300 dpi and 3mm bleed. And it is about nine weeks to records in hand including the physical test pressing, the same nine weeks at a hundred copies or a thousand, so anybody quoting three weeks is quoting a best case that will not happen. No account is needed to use it.

Admin

A separate internal dashboard for the operations side. Orders, payouts, disputes, refunds, entitlement overrides, moderation queues and reconciliation. Everything in it has to be auditable, which shaped the data model more than any other requirement.

What it costs

One number. Ten percent of a sale, and that is the entire platform fee. No monthly fee, no listing fee, no subscription to keep a store online, and no paid tier holding features back. Stripe’s processing comes out of the artist’s remaining ninety percent, which is why a ten dollar sale lands at $8.41 rather than $9.00, and the site says that up front rather than letting people find it in a statement later.

The exceptions both go the artist’s way. Tickets are a flat dollar per paid ticket instead of a percentage, so selling out a bigger room does not cost proportionally more. Merch has no platform cut at all, the artist just keeps the margin over cost. Fanclubs are free on both sides.

The stack

API is NestJS on TypeORM and Postgres. Web is Next.js 15 on the App Router with Tailwind. There are two native iOS apps in SwiftUI, the fan app and the door scanner, deliberately not cross-platform, because playback, purchase and scanning at a door all need to feel native. Payments and payouts run on Stripe Connect. Email is transactional through Resend. Media sits in Cloudflare R2. The whole thing started on a process manager and moved to containers.

How the backend is organized

It is a modular monolith, and the modules are kept honest. Commerce, entitlements, payouts and community are separate concerns that do not bleed into each other.

Access is entitlement-based. Every purchase produces an entitlement, and every access check asks the entitlement layer, not the payment provider. That keeps playback and download logic from getting welded to Stripe specifics, which matters when a web client, a fan iOS app and a door scanner are all reading the same rights.

Money is append-only. The finance and event logs are write-only ledgers, so refunds, payout changes, entitlement overrides and moderation actions all leave a trail you can replay. The payout balance is a ledger computation, credits minus debits, not a mutable number someone can fat-finger.

The payout model took real thought. It runs on Stripe Connect with the platform as merchant of record, daily automatic payouts once an artist clears a minimum, a short hold from their first sale as fraud protection, and dispute clawbacks that pull from the next payout. The artist keeps 90 percent, the platform takes 10, and the per-payout fee math is designed so the platform never pays out at a loss on a tiny balance.

The infrastructure

FIRSTPRESS runs on a self-managed Hetzner footprint, not a managed cloud. Bare-metal and cloud VMs run the containers, with my own load balancing on HAProxy, my own monitoring, and my own autoscaling. More operational work, far less cost than the hyperscalers at this scale, and the savings go into the build instead of an AWS invoice.

The shape is straightforward. Cloudflare sits at the edge for CDN, WAF, DDoS, and a tunnel, so the origin has zero open ports. HAProxy on Hetzner does health-checked L4 and L7 load balancing across the VM pool. Web (Next.js SSR), API (NestJS, blue/green deploys), and workers (video transcode, email, async jobs) each run as autoscaled container pools. Postgres is self-hosted with a primary and read replicas, point-in-time recovery via WAL-G to R2, and automated backups across zones. Media lives in Cloudflare R2, S3-compatible with zero egress fees. Secrets sit in self-hosted Vault. There are separate dev and production environments with their own databases, so I can break things on dev without touching anyone real. Fonts are self-hosted rather than pulled from a CDN, for privacy and to dodge a content-security headache.

Observability is a self-hosted Grafana stack. Prometheus for metrics, Loki for logs, Tempo for traces, and Sentry for errors tagged to the exact commit SHA, so every exception resolves back to a line of code and a deploy. The architecture is deliberately portable. Standard Postgres, Linux, and OCI containers mean it can move to any hyperscaler in a weekend if a contract or regulation ever demands it.

The CI/CD pipeline and the devops

This is the part a sloppy build would die on, so it is locked down.

It runs on GitHub Actions with self-hosted runners. The pipeline runs lint and typecheck, then end-to-end tests, then a build stage that produces the web image and runs browser tests and an audit, then deploy. Around the deploy itself are the things that keep you employed. A database backup before anything ships, migrations, then a smoke test and a schema verification after, and an automatic rollback if any of that comes back wrong.

The workflow is trunk-based on main. Squash merge only so the history stays linear and every commit on main is revertible on its own. Branches live less than a day. Anything not ready hides behind a feature flag. Main is protected with required status checks, and merges are serialized, because the self-hosted runners share ports and a test database, so two deploys racing each other is a real way to corrupt state. Capacity is deliberately kept at one for that reason.

The bug that justified all of it

The payout path had a guaranteed double-pay in it, and the unit suite was green the whole time.

A column type mismatch let the Stripe Connect transfer fire while the ledger insert failed silently. Money left, nothing recorded it leaving, and the next daily payout run would have re-paid every artist on the platform. The tests passed because they mocked the database, which is exactly the seam the mismatch lived in.

Nothing crashed. There was no exception to catch, no alert to fire, no error rate to notice. It was found by deliberately attacking the money path looking for a failure that leaves no trace, which is a different activity from writing tests and has to be scheduled as its own piece of work. That single bug is most of the argument for the append-only ledger, for integration tests that touch a real database on anything financial, and for treating the payout path as its own review category.

What is worth stealing from this

Make money append-only from the first commit, because a balance you can compute from a ledger is a balance you can defend and a mutable number never is. Never mock the boundary you are actually testing, especially where money crosses it. The plausible-but-wrong failure, the one that crashes nothing and alerts nobody, is the one worth hunting on purpose. Keep an escape hatch in the infrastructure by staying on boring, portable pieces. And be honest in the product about the parts that are unglamorous, the Stripe fee, the nine week vinyl lead time, the hundred copy floor, because the trust that buys is worth more than the sale a rounded-up number wins.
